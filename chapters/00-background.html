1  Background and starting literature

This chapter says what PRAVAH borrows, from whom, and what it does differently. It is a map, not a review: no paper listed here has been reproduced by PRAVAH unless a later chapter says so. Bibliographic details were checked against OpenAlex/arXiv (see references.bib); the descriptions below come from docs/research/literature-map.md and docs/research/green-2006-reading-notes.md.

1.1 Green (2006): the starting point

Green’s Rice University master’s thesis (Green 2006) is the methodological root of PRAVAH.

What it does.

  • Estimates unknown parameters of a dynamical system obtained by spatially discretising a parameterised transport PDE (finite elements), from sensor readings (reading notes, PDF pp. 17–29).
  • Uses weighted least squares; for Gaussian noise with covariance R and sensitivity G = ∂y/∂θ the local Fisher information is M(S) = GᵀR⁻¹G, and readings at independent times add information. With M nonsingular, M⁻¹ approximates the estimator covariance (Appendix A, PDF pp. 56–59).
  • Chooses sensor locations in advance with optimal-design criteria: D-optimality (maximise log det M, i.e. minimise the volume of the confidence ellipsoid), A-optimality (minimise trace M⁻¹) and E-type criteria; it also formulates a weighted approximate design over discrete candidate locations (PDF pp. 34–46).
  • Illustrates this on a 1-D time-dependent advection–diffusion problem with two unknowns, diffusivity and advection coefficient. Optimised sensors land near x ≈ 0.025 and 0.975 on [0, 1]; the 95 % confidence-ellipse area is ~1.91e-3 (centre placement), ~4.66e-4 (equal spacing) and ~2.97e-4 (optimised), i.e. ~36 % below equal spacing (PDF p. 54).

What it does not do.

  • It does not recover a concentration field or many unknown source strengths; it identifies a handful of transport parameters.
  • It does not treat low-cost sensor noise, correlated errors, held-out meteorology, or forecasting.
  • The boundary-sensor result is specific to that PDE, parameter vector and noise model; it is not a rule for placing pollution sensors.
  • Its asymptotic Fisher confidence regions assume a correctly specified model; under wind or source misspecification their coverage may be poor (PRAVAH gate-04 found exactly this kind of miscalibration when the noise model is wrong).

How PRAVAH adapts it. The unknowns become K non-negative source strengths with known footprints; G stacks per-source responses over sensors and hours; we use a Bayesian D-criterion log det(I + a_ref² GᵀR⁻¹G) so that singular designs remain comparable; and the decisive score is not the confidence ellipse but held-out forecast error. Every 2-D source-strength extension is a PRAVAH adaptation, not a reproduction. The thesis PDF is a scan without extractable text, so the page pointers above come from the reading notes and should be checked against the original.

1.2 Related work and how each paper is used

Transport models and footprints.

  • Pennington et al. (2024) build and evaluate a CMAQ chemistry-transport framework for Los Angeles with source apportionment. It is a reference point for what a full 3-D chemical model includes that our passive tracer omits (E01, later E13/E17).
  • Schlutow et al. (2026) present BLDFM, a fast Eulerian steady-state advection–diffusion solver for concentration and flux footprints. It motivates the footprint view of E04; its code is non-commercially licensed and is not copied.

Source reconstruction and identifiability.

  • Mons et al. (2017) reconstruct urban pollutant release characteristics by data assimilation. It is the closest analogue to the E03 inverse and to the wind-mismatch experiments planned for plan 3.
  • Bhardwaj and Subramanian (2026) (arXiv preprint, not peer reviewed) study identifiability-aware source apportionment in city-scale advection–diffusion systems. It shaped the protocol’s separation of identifiability from prediction accuracy (E00, E04, E05). We cite it as a preprint only.

Sensor placement.

  • Manohar et al. (2018) introduce data-driven sparse placement by POD modes and QR pivoting; Silva et al. (2021) and Karnik et al. (2026) provide the PySensors implementation. PRAVAH’s podqr design follows this, and its QR pivots were checked against PySensors 0.4.3 (E06).
  • Zhou et al. (2022) apply POD-based planning to PM2.5 monitoring sites across China, with sensor-count and mode-count ablations (relevant to E06 and E08).
  • Mano et al. (2022) solve air-pollution sensor location–allocation with information theory; Krause et al. (2008) give near-optimal mutual-information placement for Gaussian processes with submodularity guarantees. Both inform the information criteria in E06/E07 and robust placement (E15). Note that the greedy guarantees need not hold with a binding spacing constraint (gate-06).
  • Choi and Hummel (2025) place low-cost PM2.5 sensors by value of information with multimodal data fusion. It is the closest low-cost-network analogue to PRAVAH’s question (E07, E11, E15). (The literature map lists it as 2026; the publisher record gives 2025.)
  • Bartos and Kerkez (2021) use observability-based placement for contaminant tracing in river networks: the control-theoretic view behind E04/E05 (observability over time) and E11.

Sequential estimation (plan 2 E09–E11).

  • Roy et al. (2018) combine a kriged Kalman filter with sparsity-enforcing placement.
  • Zheng et al. (2009) update the state and parameters of an atmospheric dispersion model online with an ensemble Kalman filter (listed as enkf2009 in the literature map).
  • Sharma et al. (2019) estimate contaminant distributions from sparse sensors with a Perron–Frobenius operator and EnKF.
  • Cheng and Paley (2022) estimate a 2-D advection–diffusion process with mobile sensors and optimal guidance.
  • Raanes et al. (2024) (DAPPER) is the planned reference implementation for data-assimilation cross-checks.

Meteorological uncertainty (plan 3).

  • Webster and Thomson (2022) treat meteorological uncertainty in Bayesian volcanic-ash inversion with ensemble meteorology: the template for E12 (wind mismatch) and E14 (calibration).
  • Mues et al. (2018) simulate meteorology and black carbon in the Kathmandu Valley with WRF/WRF-Chem: context for the optional regional case (E17).

Not cited. The literature map also lists a gradient-based mutual-information placement repository (Patel et al., 2026, GitHub only); it has no verifiable bibliographic record and is treated as unverified. Data products (ERA5, FIRMS, WorldPop) concern the optional regional case and are not used in this report.

1.3 The gap PRAVAH addresses

Placement studies usually score a design on the same information criterion that chose it, or on in-sample reconstruction. PRAVAH asks whether a design chosen from training winds still helps on held-out winds, with noisy sensors and several unknown sources, judged by a forecast endpoint fixed before the data were seen.
