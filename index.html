PRAVAH: sparse low-cost sensor placement under uncertain wind

Plan 1–2 research report (E00–E07), synthetic 2-D benchmark

Published

November 13, 2026

Executive summary

ImportantScope

Everything in this report is a synthetic study: a 2-D, depth-averaged passive tracer simulated with FiPy on a 10 km × 10 km square, with known truth. It is not PM2.5, not validated against observations, and makes no real-world exposure or deployment claim. Most results are exploratory; one test (E07) is confirmatory, and only for the cells that were run.

The question

How few low-cost, noisy sensors do we need, and where should they go, to reconstruct and forecast a pollution field when the wind and the emissions are uncertain? Plan 2 (plans/plan-02-identifiability-placement-forecast.yaml) breaks this into: which source combinations a network can actually distinguish (identifiability), whether a longer history can replace sensors, which placement rule is best for which task, and finally sequential forecasting (E08–E11).

Where we started: Green (2006)

Our methodological starting point is Kary Green’s Rice master’s thesis on optimal sensor placement for parameter identification (Green 2006). Green linearises sensor readings around the parameters of a semi-discretised PDE, forms the Fisher information matrix M = GᵀR⁻¹G, and chooses sensor locations that maximise log det M (D-optimal design). His worked example is a 1-D advection–diffusion problem with two unknown parameters (diffusivity and advection coefficient); the optimised design shrinks the 95 % confidence-ellipse area by about 36 % relative to equal spacing (reading notes, docs/research/green-2006-reading-notes.md).

PRAVAH keeps the Fisher/D-optimal machinery but changes almost everything around it:

Green (2006) PRAVAH
Space 1-D 2-D, 64 × 64 grid (32² for pilots)
Unknowns 2 transport parameters K = 2–8 source strengths (plus, later, the field)
Sensors ideal, few low-cost, noise σ = 5 % of median concentration; AR(1) and per-site arms
Wind known, fixed time-varying; test winds held out from design
Endpoint parameter confidence ellipse held-out one-hour-ahead field forecast RMSE (protocol primary)
Truth analytic synthetic simulator with known truth, independent audits

The testbed

A FiPy finite-volume solver of ∂c/∂t + u·∇c = ∇·(K∇c) − λ(c − c_bg) + S, with K = 50 m² s⁻¹, λ = 1/12 h, Gaussian area sources (σ = 400 m), background 5 µg m⁻³, hourly-piecewise wind, a 60-day calendar split into train (days 1–30), validation (31–42) and test (43–60) with held-out test wind regimes. Sensors sit on a 16 × 16 candidate lattice with 1.25 km minimum spacing. Every experiment received an independent audit (“gate”).

Key figures

What we learned, in plain language

  1. Scale matters before cleverness. At the originally declared emission rate, sensors could not see the plumes at all; no placement rule can fix that (E00–E05, amendment A1).
  2. Numerics matter. The first-order scheme smeared plumes with 7–60× the physical diffusivity at 64²; switching to a split VanLeer scheme was necessary before any 64² result could mean anything (A2).
  3. Time helps only when the wind changes. Repeated readings under the same wind reduce noise but do not separate sources; a change in wind direction does (E05, exploratory).
  4. Optimised placement beats random, confirmed in the 8 cells run (E07).
  5. But the gain comes from knowing where the sources are, not from D-optimisation: simply placing sensors next to the known sources did better still, and D-optimal networks tuned to training winds failed when an unseen (southerly) wind blew in the test period: 79 % of their forecast error falls in those blind windows (E07, exploratory).

Honest limitations

  • Synthetic, perfect-model world: known source locations, known wind, constant sources, ideal sensors, uniform wind.
  • Smoke scale (32²) for E02–E06; only E07 ran at 64² and only for K ∈ {2, 4}, k ∈ {4, 8, 16}, 10 seeds.
  • The source scale (1 g/s) was chosen after seeing data, so E02–E06 at that scale are exploratory.
  • The E07 mechanism rests on one unseen wind direction; the “source-near” advantage may partly reflect sensors sitting inside the 1200 m emission disc. Across all 40 scenarios, excluding those sites keeps near-source ahead (104/120) but shrinks D-optimal’s gain over random from 71 % to 20 %, and no single cell then clears the 10 % test threshold (exploratory control).
  • No sequential filter, no wind error, no model mismatch yet: that is E09–E15.
